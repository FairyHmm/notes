> The graph below shows the percentage of workers from five different European countries that were absent for a day or more due to illness from 1991 to 2001

![c3c7cbd6b9d8450c9a423505dd0276c7.png](/img/user/resources/c3c7cbd6b9d8450c9a423505dd0276c7.png)

The line graph compares how the percentage of people taking a day off from work because of sickness for one or multiple days differ, between 1991 and 2001 in five distinct European nations.

Overall, Germany had the lowest absence rate, while the Netherlands was the opposite. Also, the figures seem to be more stable the lower they are.

In 1991, the Netherlands had just under 5% of its workforce taking at least a sick day, just barely below France's statistics. With a jump in the next year, the percentage of Dutch being absence rose to 5.5%, bumping it to the first place. In the next four years, a declining trend for the Netherlands' data was observed, reaching only 4% in 1996. For the rest of the research, however, the percentage of workers taking sick day gradually grew again, almost matching the 1992's value. Both the UK and Germany had fluctuations in their statistics, albeit minimally. The highest values for the UK was 2.2% in 1992 and 2.8% in 1997, respectively. And for Germany, the percentage of sick employees being off from work stayed between 1.4% (1991) and 1.9% (1998).

France and Sweden experienced opposite tendencies. From an initial 3%, Swedish workers took increasingly more sick leaves, while French employees did the reverse. Two lines eventually crossed at 1996, at just less than 3.5%. Afterwards, the percentage of workers in Sweden who had taken sick days accelerated by another 1% in the last year of the research, while the figure for France stabilised at under 3%.
